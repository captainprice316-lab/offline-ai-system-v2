\title{What Actually Improves Low-Resource ASR:\\
Lessons from Twenty LoRA Adaptations Across Eight Border Languages}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
We report the two-month adaptation campaign behind a deployed, fully offline
speech-recognition system for eight low-resource border languages, comprising
roughly twenty LoRA fine-tuning runs across two multilingual backbones. The
campaign's headline outcome is a complete backend replacement: per-language
fine-tuned Whisper, the system's original design, now serves \emph{none} of the
eight languages, having been displaced by a single shared SeamlessM4T~v2 model
with per-language adapters.

Two of the eight languages---Kashmiri and Dogri---are absent from both backbones'
language inventories, and we show that the resulting failures are
representational rather than acoustic. A language a model cannot name is not
rejected but silently misrouted: presented with Dogri audio, Whisper's language
identification answers \emph{Punjabi} for 222 of 425 clips and emits Gurmukhi
against Devanagari references, yielding a word error rate above 100\% that no
accuracy dashboard would flag as a missing-language problem. We add the missing
language token, initialised from a neighbour and trained, and take Dogri from
102.25\% to 46.73\% WER---the largest single-language gain of the campaign, in a
language that had never previously been measured.

Applying the same technique to Kashmiri exposed a second and subtler defect: 20
characters of written Kashmiri have no token in the backbone's subword
vocabulary and resolve to the unknown-token id, appearing in 96.9\% of training
sentences. Of the 747 test words containing the four affected letters, the
pre-repair model got \emph{all 747} wrong; after repair it recovers 310. We
quantify each lever the campaign tried, and find that a retrospective paired
bootstrap divides them sharply: backend replacement, vocabulary repair and
training to convergence are significant at $p<0.001$, whereas every
adapter-versus-adapter decision---including one on which a deployment was
made---is indistinguishable from noise on the 30- and 100-clip evaluations used
to gate them. We close by measuring the deployed code path directly and finding
it 2.07~pp worse than the path that produced every number we had published.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most practical work on low-resource speech recognition assumes the target
language is at least \emph{representable} by the model being adapted---that it
has a language token, and that its script survives the subword vocabulary
intact. For the languages that motivate this paper, neither assumption holds,
and the consequences are not the ones the literature prepares you for.

We describe the adaptation campaign behind a deployed offline speech pipeline
for eight border-region languages: Punjabi, Pashto, Urdu, Nepali, Mandarin,
Hindi, Kashmiri and Dogri. The system runs air-gapped on a single workstation,
transcribing degraded radio audio and translating it to English. Its original
design fine-tuned one Whisper model per language. Over roughly twenty adaptation
runs we replaced that design entirely, and in doing so accumulated a set of
results that we believe are more useful to practitioners than the final error
rates: several of the things that obviously should have helped did not, one that
looked like a modelling problem turned out to be a vocabulary bug, and a
retrospective statistical audit showed that a substantial fraction of the
decisions we made along the way were unsupported by the evaluations we used to
make them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A backend-selection study across eight languages showing that a single
shared multilingual model with per-language LoRA adapters outperforms
per-language fine-tuned Whisper everywhere, once three independent scoring
defects in the original comparison are corrected (Section~\ref{sec:backend}).

\item A method for languages the backbone does not support---adding a language
token initialised from a linguistic neighbour and training it---demonstrated on
two languages with different scripts and different failure profiles. We show
that the correct neighbour is determined by \emph{script}, not genetic
relatedness, and settle this by measurement (Section~\ref{sec:unsupported}).

\item The identification and repair of a subword-vocabulary defect that placed a
hard floor under Kashmiri accuracy: 20 characters resolving to the unknown token
across 96.9\% of training sentences, rendering 747 of 747 affected test words
unwinnable regardless of model capacity or training duration
(Section~\ref{sec:vocab}).

\item A quantified ranking of every lever the campaign tried, including three
that produced no significant improvement, and a retrospective significance audit
showing that our 30-clip robustness gate could not resolve the differences it
was used to decide (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).

\item A direct measurement of the deployed code path against the evaluation
harness, which differ by 2.07~pp---a gap we report rather than conceal, because
it is the clearest instance of this paper's recurring theme
(Section~\ref{sec:deployed}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A note on what this paper is.} This is an experience report, and its
value lies substantially in the negative results. We deployed an adapter on a
0.75~pp margin we later found to be insignificant; we rejected two others on
differences that were also insignificant; we spent a day pursuing a decoding
improvement that turned out to trade clean-speech accuracy for robustness at low
signal-to-noise ratio, which is the wrong direction for a radio system. Each of
these is reported with the evidence that undermines it. We think this is more
useful than a clean narrative, because the conditions that produced them---small
evaluation sets, sub-2~pp effects, a gate inherited from an earlier phase of the
work---are entirely ordinary.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

The system transcribes intercepted radio traffic offline. Every model runs
locally on one workstation with an 8~GB consumer GPU; no audio leaves the
machine, which rules out hosted APIs and constrains model size throughout. Audio
arrives degraded---narrowband, noisy, codec-compressed---so clean-speech accuracy
alone is never a sufficient basis for a deployment decision, a point that
recurs in Sections~\ref{sec:measure} and \ref{sec:decode}.

\paragraph{Languages and data.} Six of the eight languages have FLEURS test
splits, which we use at $n=100$ per language. Kashmiri and Dogri do not appear in
FLEURS at all; for these we use the IndicVoices-R test splits, 372 and 425 clips
respectively. This asymmetry matters more than it first appears, and
Section~\ref{sec:measure} shows that the 100-clip splits cannot resolve the
differences the campaign spent most of its effort chasing.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Four of the eight languages are written in Perso-Arabic or
Devanagari scripts whose orthographic conventions make raw word error rate
misleading. We therefore score on a normalisation ladder, from raw text (L0)
through Unicode NFC and whitespace normalisation (L1), diacritic stripping (L2),
and conservative and aggressive character folding (L3, L4). We report L2 as the
deciding level throughout. The choice is consequential for Kashmiri, where L0 and
L2 differ by roughly 14~pp because Perso-Arabic references are densely
diacritised and no model reproduces those marks; it is nearly irrelevant for
Dogri, where L1 through L4 are identical because Devanagari carries no comparable
burden. A single scorer is used for every language and every system, including
character segmentation for Mandarin.

\paragraph{Adaptation.} All adaptation is LoRA. Whisper runs used rank 8--16;
SeamlessM4T adapters ranged from rank 8 to rank 128 on the attention and MLP
projections, the upper end requiring rented cloud GPUs because rank 128 exceeds
the 8~GB workstation. Total cloud expenditure across the campaign was
approximately \$25.

Table~\ref{tab:headline} gives the outcome. Fine-tuned Whisper now serves none of

of those required more than an adapter.

\paragraph{Three scoring defects preceded the result.} The original comparison
favoured Whisper, and the reversal came from correcting the measurement rather
than from any change to the models. First, the ``untuned baseline'' loaded
\texttt{whisper-large-v3-turbo} rather than \texttt{large-v3}, a different model
with different behaviour. Second, Mandarin word error rate was computed without
character segmentation, so unspaced Han transcripts scored near 100\% regardless
of content---an artefact that had been reported as a headline improvement from
100\% to 9\%. Third, a label-encoding bug in the speech-to-text-translation
path depressed one backend's scores independently of its recognition quality.
Each defect had produced a plausible number that survived review because nothing
about it looked wrong.

We note this not as an aside but because it establishes the pattern the rest of
the paper documents: in this project, the majority of apparent model findings
that we investigated closely turned out to be measurement findings.

\section{The Adaptation Campaign}
\label{sec:campaign}

Across roughly twenty runs, the levers we tried divide cleanly into those that
moved word error rate and those that did not.

\paragraph{Pashto: seven attempts, one real gain.} Pashto was fine-tuned
Whisper's last stronghold and took the most effort. Adding Common Voice data to
FLEURS in a mixture dominated by the former \emph{regressed} accuracy by 5.6~pp
($p<0.001$), a domain-drift effect. Rebalancing the mixture and raising adapter
rank recovered it. The decisive change was neither data nor capacity but
\emph{noise-augmented training}: degrading the training audio with the same
bandpass, additive-noise and codec pipeline used for evaluation produced an
adapter that both improved clean speech and, more importantly, stopped collapsing
at low signal-to-noise ratio. Two later attempts---scaling the Common Voice share
threefold, and raising rank to 128---produced changes we cannot distinguish from
noise (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).

\paragraph{Kashmiri: eight versions across two backbones.} Kashmiri began at
74.02\% and ended at 50.26\%. The path there involved a custom language token
(Section~\ref{sec:unsupported}), a fourfold corpus expansion, a rank increase
from 32 to 128, a vocabulary repair (Section~\ref{sec:vocab}), and one run that
simply trained longer.

\paragraph{Training to convergence is worth more than it appears.} Two runs in
this campaign stopped not because they had converged but because their linear
learning-rate schedule had decayed to zero. Both recorded their best validation
loss at their final step, having improved at essentially every evaluation.
Restarting each from its own weights with a fresh schedule---changing nothing
else---improved Kashmiri by 3.84~pp and Dogri by 3.34~pp, both significant at
$p<0.001$. The two languages differ in script, corpus and backbone history, and
the effect replicates across them at almost the same magnitude. A schedule that
expires produces a loss curve indistinguishable from convergence, and the cost of
mistaking one for the other was, in this campaign, larger than the cost of
doubling adapter rank.

\section{Languages the Model Cannot Name}
\label{sec:unsupported}

Kashmiri and Dogri are absent from both backbones' language inventories.
SeamlessM4T~v2 ships 101 language tokens, including \texttt{\_\_hin\_\_},
\texttt{\_\_pan\_\_} and \texttt{\_\_urd\_\_}, but neither \texttt{\_\_kas\_\_}
nor \texttt{\_\_doi\_\_}; Whisper has no \texttt{<|ks|>} or \texttt{<|doi|>}.

\paragraph{The failure is silent.} A model given a language it cannot name does
not refuse. It routes the input to whatever it considers nearest. Presented with
Dogri audio, Whisper's language identification answers \emph{Punjabi} for 222 of
425 clips, against 25 for Hindi---a linguistically defensible judgement, since
Dogri is far closer to Punjabi than to Hindi. It then transcribes in
\textbf{Gurmukhi}, while Dogri is written in Devanagari. The resulting word error
rate exceeds 100\%, because insertions outnumber the words that could have been
matched. Nothing in the pipeline reports an error; the system produces confident,
fluent, entirely unusable output, and did so for the entire period Dogri was
nominally supported.

\paragraph{Method.} We add the missing token to the tokeniser, initialise its
embedding from the closest supported language, and---critically---make that
embedding trainable, using PEFT's trainable-token mechanism to learn a delta on
that row alone while the rest of the embedding table stays frozen. Every earlier
Kashmiri attempt had left the initialised row frozen, treating it as a fixed
conditioning vector; that choice was the difference between an unusable model
(129\% WER) and a deployable one.

\paragraph{Which neighbour? Script, not ancestry.}
\label{par:neighbour}
The initialisation source is a real design decision, and the intuitive answer is
wrong. Dogri's nearest relative among the supported languages is Punjabi, and
Whisper's language identification agrees emphatically. But SeamlessM4T knows
Punjabi only in Gurmukhi, and a language token conditions \emph{generation}, not
recognition. Table~\ref{tab:dogri} settles it by measurement: zero-shot decoding
with the Punjabi token scores 114.62\% WER against 99.99\% for Hindi, and the
character error rates---96.81\% versus 68.14\%---show why. The Punjabi prior
emits the wrong script, and almost nothing matches. Recognition and generation
want different neighbours; the token serves generation, so script wins.

The result is a 55~pp improvement over what the deployed system actually did, and
41~pp over the best baseline obtainable without training. It is the largest
single-language gain of the campaign, and it came from a language nobody had
measured, using a technique already in the codebase for another language. The
same method, unchanged, works on two languages with different scripts and
different failure profiles---which is our basis for calling it a method rather
than a fix.

\section{A Floor Made of Missing Characters}
\label{sec:vocab}

Applying the technique to Kashmiri exposed a second defect, invisible to every
diagnostic we routinely used.

After deploying a rank-128 Kashmiri adapter at 56.44\% WER, we examined the
composition of the remaining errors rather than the aggregate. Substitutions
accounted for 77\% of them, the hypothesis-to-reference length ratio was 0.943,
and no output was truncated---so the early-termination pathology that had
afflicted earlier Kashmiri models was gone, and little was recoverable by
adjusting decoding. The errors were words the model wrote incorrectly, not words
it failed to write.

Inspecting the most frequent confusions showed pairs differing by a single
character: \texttt{p\=a\d{t}h\u{i}} for \texttt{p\'a\d{t}h\u{i}},
\texttt{xa\d{t}r} for \texttt{x\'a\d{t}r}. Those characters are Kashmiri-specific
vowel signs. Testing them against the tokeniser gave the explanation: they encode
to the \textbf{unknown token}.

\paragraph{Extent.} Twenty characters used in written Kashmiri have no token in
SeamlessM4T's subword vocabulary. They occur 854{,}234 times in the training
corpus and appear in \textbf{96.9\%} of its sentences. Two consequences follow.
The model cannot emit them---one such character appears 370 times in the test
references and \emph{zero} times in any hypothesis. And because the same
tokeniser encodes the training targets, the model was trained against corrupted
labels for nearly every sentence it saw.

\paragraph{Cost.} Of the 3{,}917 substitution errors, 662 fall on words rendered
unrepresentable by these characters. Of the 747 test words containing the four
affected \emph{letters}---the subset that survives diacritic normalisation---the
model got \textbf{747 wrong. Every one.} No amount of capacity, data or training
time could have fixed a single one of them.

\paragraph{Repair and result.} We add all twenty characters as tokens,
initialise each from its closest in-vocabulary neighbour (letters from their base
letter, combining marks from a similar mark) and train those rows alongside the
language token. Nothing else changes: same corpus, same rank, same schedule, same
converged step, so the comparison isolates a single variable. Word error rate
falls from 52.60\% to \textbf{50.26\%} ($p<0.001$), and of the 747 previously
unwinnable words the repaired model recovers \textbf{310}. The remaining failures
are ordinary acoustic confusions between similar words rather than blank
omissions.

\paragraph{Why the aggregate gain is smaller than the defect.} On unnormalised
text the repair is worth 9.60~pp (74.31\% to 64.71\%); at L2 it is worth 2.34~pp.
Most of the twenty characters are combining marks that diacritic normalisation
discards anyway, so the deciding ruler sees only the four letters. Both numbers
are real, and reporting only the larger would misrepresent what the repair buys
under the metric we actually decide on.

\paragraph{Generality.} This defect is not exotic. It arises whenever a
language's written form uses characters outside the coverage of a subword
vocabulary trained predominantly on other languages, which is the ordinary
condition for scripts with language-specific extensions. It is invisible to loss
curves, because the model learns to predict the unknown token perfectly well; it
is invisible to aggregate WER, because the affected words simply appear as
ordinary errors; and it is invisible to any evaluation that does not inspect
which characters the model is capable of producing. Dogri, by contrast, is
written in unextended Devanagari and shows \emph{zero} unsupported characters,
which is why it needed only the language token.

\section{What the Evaluation Could Not See}
\label{sec:measure}

Having reached deployable accuracy on all eight languages, we ran a
retrospective audit: for every comparison on which a decision had been taken, a
paired bootstrap over clips (10{,}000 resamples, re-aggregating errors and
reference length rather than averaging per-clip rates). The pairing matters,
since both systems transcribe identical audio and removing clip-difficulty
variance is what makes small effects detectable at all.

The audit divided the campaign along a line we had not anticipated
(Table~\ref{tab:sig}).

The split follows test-set size and effect size together. Every conclusion about
\emph{backend selection}---the question the project originally asked---is
supported, because replacing one model family with another moves word error rate
by 10 to 22~pp and even 30 clips resolve that comfortably. So are the two
mechanisms established on the 372- and 425-clip Kashmiri and Dogri sets.

What does not survive is the fine-grained adapter selection that consumed most of
the campaign's effort. The 1 to 3~pp differences between an adapter and its
successor, evaluated on a 30-clip robustness sweep and a 100-clip clean split,
are indistinguishable from noise in every case we tested---including the 0.75~pp
margin on which we deployed one adapter over another.

\paragraph{``Wins 4 of 5 conditions'' is not a result.} Our deployment gate
reported robustness as a win count across five degradation conditions, a format
that reads like evidence. Decomposed, the four wins are four differences whose
confidence intervals all cross zero, and the count itself has a one-sided sign
test $p=0.188$. The gate was designed during earlier work when the differences
under test were tens of points, and it was never revisited as those differences
shrank to fractions of one. A gate adequate for choosing a backbone is not
automatically adequate for choosing between two checkpoints of the same model.

\paragraph{What we changed, and what we did not.} We did not reverse the
deployments. Choosing the newer of two statistically indistinguishable adapters
is defensible under uncertainty, and one does not replace a running model without
evidence of improvement. What changed is the claim: those adapters are
\emph{equivalent on the available evidence}, not better. We report this because
the alternative---quietly restating point estimates as findings---is precisely
the practice that produced three of this paper's corrections.

\section{Decoding: a Trade, Not an Improvement}
\label{sec:decode}

Late in the campaign we noticed that the deployed system decodes greedily: the
backbone ships no beam-search default and the serving code never sets one, so all
eight languages had been decoding with a single beam without anyone choosing
that. Beam search with width 8 improves Kashmiri clean word error rate from
50.26\% to 47.37\%, and adding an in-domain Kneser--Ney trigram language model to
rescore the 8-best list reaches 46.90\%, against an oracle ceiling of 44.33\%
over that list. Wall-clock cost is about 5\%, not the eightfold one might expect,
because decode time is dominated by a sequential step count that a
minimum-length constraint fixes and the GPU absorbs the wider batch.

We did not deploy it. Across eight configurations---beam widths 2, 4 and 8, each
with length penalties 1.0, 0.8 and 0.6---\emph{every one} regressed the 0~dB
signal-to-noise condition by 2 to 3~pp while gaining 2 to 3~pp on clean audio.
Length penalty was our hypothesis for the cause and is not: it recovers about
0.5~pp of the regression. With flat posteriors, beam search reliably finds
higher-scoring hypotheses that are fluent and wrong, and greedy decoding's myopia
is a virtue exactly when the acoustic evidence is weakest. For a system whose
purpose is degraded radio, that trade runs the wrong way.

The general form of this is worth stating: \textbf{decode settings validated only
on clean test sets can silently degrade deployed robustness}, and the clean-speech
number---the one a paper would normally report---moves in the flattering
direction.

\section{The Deployed Path Is Not the Evaluated Path}
\label{sec:deployed}

A final measurement closes the paper because it undercuts the rest of it.

Every word error rate we have reported was produced by our evaluation harness,
which constructs the model with the adapter loaded through the parameter-efficient
fine-tuning library. The deployed system constructs it differently: it bakes the
trainable-token embedding deltas into the base model and loads only the low-rank
matrices, because the library cannot stack a trainable-token delta alongside
several named adapters. The two constructions were assumed equivalent for a year
and never compared.

They are not. Measured on the same 372 clips with the same scorer, Kashmiri
scores \textbf{52.33\%} through the deployed path against \textbf{50.26\%}
through the evaluation path: a 2.07~pp gap, 95\% CI $[+1.24, +2.91]$,
$p<0.001$, with only 19 of 372 hypotheses identical between them. The deployed
model is measurably worse than the model this paper describes, and because the
divergence lives in the shared generation path rather than in anything
language-specific, the other seven languages are presumed affected and have
simply never been measured both ways.

Investigating it fixed one genuine defect---the deployment baked only the first
of 21 trainable-token deltas, leaving the twenty repaired characters of
Section~\ref{sec:vocab} at their initialisation values in production. The
residual 2~pp is unresolved. Direct comparison has excluded the low-rank weights
(480 of 480 tensors identical), the deltas, the extracted input features, the
generation length limit, the input dtype, substituting the checkpoint's frozen
embedding table, and multi-adapter interference.

We report this rather than correcting it silently. Republishing every number
against the deployed path would require re-evaluating eight languages, and the
gap is in any case the clearest instance of what this paper keeps finding: the
artefact you measure is not necessarily the artefact you ship.

\section{Lessons}
\label{sec:lessons}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Check what your model can emit before adapting it.} A tokeniser
audit costs minutes and would have found the 20-character defect a month earlier.
Loss curves, aggregate WER and validation accuracy are all blind to it.

\item \textbf{A missing language fails silently.} Absent a language token, a
model routes to its nearest neighbour and produces confident, well-formed,
useless output. Verify that every language you claim to support is one the model
can name.

\item \textbf{Initialise custom language tokens by script, not by ancestry.}
The token conditions generation; a genetically closer language in the wrong
script starts you further away.

\item \textbf{A schedule that expires is not a converged model.} Two runs here
left 3--4~pp on the table by stopping when their learning rate reached zero.

\item \textbf{Size the evaluation to the effect.} A 30-clip sweep resolves
backend replacement and nothing finer. If you are chasing 1~pp, report intervals
or stop claiming improvements.

\item \textbf{Measure the deployed path.} Evaluation and serving code diverge
quietly, and the divergence is worth points.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We replaced a per-language fine-tuned Whisper deployment with a single shared
multilingual model and per-language adapters across eight low-resource border
languages, including two the backbone cannot name and one that had never been
measured at all. The largest gains came not from capacity or data but from
repairs to representation: a language token the model lacked, and twenty
characters its vocabulary could not express. The most useful result, though, may
be the audit: of the decisions this campaign made, those about backends were
supported by the evidence and those about individual adapters largely were not,
and we could not have known which was which without asking.
