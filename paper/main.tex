\title{What Actually Improves Low-Resource ASR:\\
Lessons from Twenty LoRA Adaptations Across Eight Border Languages}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
We report the two-month adaptation campaign behind a deployed, fully offline
speech-recognition system for eight low-resource border languages, comprising
roughly twenty LoRA fine-tuning runs across two multilingual backbones. The
campaign's headline outcome is a complete backend replacement: per-language
fine-tuned Whisper, the system's original design, now serves \emph{none} of the
eight languages, having been displaced by a single shared SeamlessM4T~v2 model
with per-language adapters.

Two of the eight languages---Kashmiri and Dogri---are absent from both backbones'
language inventories, and we show that the resulting failures are
representational rather than acoustic. A language a model cannot name is not
rejected but silently misrouted: presented with Dogri audio, Whisper's language
identification answers \emph{Punjabi} for 222 of 425 clips and emits Gurmukhi
against Devanagari references, yielding a word error rate above 100\% that no
accuracy dashboard would flag as a missing-language problem. We add the missing
language token, initialised from a neighbour and trained, and take Dogri from
102.25\% to 46.73\% WER---the largest single-language gain of the campaign, in a
language that had never previously been measured.

Applying the same technique to Kashmiri exposed a second and subtler defect: 20
characters of written Kashmiri have no token in the backbone's subword
vocabulary and resolve to the unknown-token id, appearing in 96.9\% of training
sentences. Of the 747 test words containing the four affected letters, the
pre-repair model got \emph{all 747} wrong; after repair it recovers 310. We
quantify each lever the campaign tried, and find that a retrospective paired
bootstrap divides them sharply: backend replacement, vocabulary repair and
training to convergence are significant at $p<0.001$, whereas every
adapter-versus-adapter decision---including one on which a deployment was
made---is indistinguishable from noise on the 30- and 100-clip evaluations used
to gate them. We close by measuring the deployed code path directly and finding
it 2.07~pp worse than the path that produced every number we had published.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most practical work on low-resource speech recognition assumes the target
language is at least \emph{representable} by the model being adapted---that it
has a language token, and that its script survives the subword vocabulary
intact. For the languages that motivate this paper, neither assumption holds,
and the consequences are not the ones the literature prepares you for.

We describe the adaptation campaign behind a deployed offline speech pipeline
for eight border-region languages: Punjabi, Pashto, Urdu, Nepali, Mandarin,
Hindi, Kashmiri and Dogri. The system runs air-gapped on a single workstation,
transcribing degraded radio audio and translating it to English. Its original
design fine-tuned one Whisper model per language. Over roughly twenty adaptation
runs we replaced that design entirely, and in doing so accumulated a set of
results that we believe are more useful to practitioners than the final error
rates: several of the things that obviously should have helped did not, one that
looked like a modelling problem turned out to be a vocabulary bug, and a
retrospective statistical audit showed that a substantial fraction of the
decisions we made along the way were unsupported by the evaluations we used to
make them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A backend-selection study across eight languages showing that a single
shared multilingual model with per-language LoRA adapters outperforms
per-language fine-tuned Whisper everywhere, once three independent scoring
defects in the original comparison are corrected (Section~\ref{sec:backend}).

\item A method for languages the backbone does not support---adding a language
token initialised from a linguistic neighbour and training it---demonstrated on
two languages with different scripts and different failure profiles. We show
that the correct neighbour is determined by \emph{script}, not genetic
relatedness, and settle this by measurement (Section~\ref{sec:unsupported}).

\item The identification and repair of a subword-vocabulary defect that placed a
hard floor under Kashmiri accuracy: 20 characters resolving to the unknown token
across 96.9\% of training sentences, rendering 747 of 747 affected test words
unwinnable regardless of model capacity or training duration
(Section~\ref{sec:vocab}).

\item A quantified ranking of every lever the campaign tried, including three
that produced no significant improvement, and a retrospective significance audit
showing that our 30-clip robustness gate could not resolve the differences it
was used to decide (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).

\item A direct measurement of the deployed code path against the evaluation
harness, which differ by 2.07~pp---a gap we report rather than conceal, because
it is the clearest instance of this paper's recurring theme
(Section~\ref{sec:deployed}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A note on what this paper is.} This is an experience report, and its
value lies substantially in the negative results. We deployed an adapter on a
0.75~pp margin we later found to be insignificant; we rejected two others on
differences that were also insignificant; we spent a day pursuing a decoding
improvement that turned out to trade clean-speech accuracy for robustness at low
signal-to-noise ratio, which is the wrong direction for a radio system. Each of
these is reported with the evidence that undermines it. We think this is more
useful than a clean narrative, because the conditions that produced them---small
evaluation sets, sub-2~pp effects, a gate inherited from an earlier phase of the
work---are entirely ordinary.
